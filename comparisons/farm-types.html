🏡 Complete Farm Type Comparison

Choosing a farm is the most important decision when starting your Stardew Valley journey. This guide analyzes the pros and cons of the six farm types in detail to help you make the best choice. A note first: Stardew Valley actually has seven farm types (including the "Meadowlands Farm" added in version 1.6). This article focuses on comparing the six most common mainstream farms, with a deep dive into each farm's space utilization, layout philosophy, and long-term impact.

💡 Evaluation Dimensions
  • Plantable area: the actual tillable flat land ratio after deducting water, rocks, and trees
  • Signature resources: outputs unique to that farm (e.g., the Forest Farm's hardwood stumps, the Riverland Farm's pond fish)
  • Layout friendliness: how easy it is to lay sprinklers and plan automation
  • Long-term potential: the growth ceiling and gameplay richness of that farm from year three onward

🌾 Standard Farm

✅ Pros
  • Largest space, with large flat areas for planting
  • A river runs through, convenient for fishing and irrigation
  • Balanced overall layout, suited for all-round players
  • Beginner-friendly, gentle learning curve
⚠️ Notes
  • Lacks unique signature resources
  • Requires reasonable space planning
  • Relatively less distinctive

Space utilization: The Standard Farm has a tillable area of about 3,427 tiles (after deducting water edges), the largest of all farms. What does this mean? Suppose you lay iridium sprinklers (covering 24 tiles)—the entire farm can theoretically hold 140 sprinklers, that is, 3,360 crops watered simultaneously. Even developing only half the land is enough to support a million-gold-tier wine-making industry chain.

Layout suggestions: We suggest dividing the farm into four functional zones—a central planting zone (with sprinklers), a southwest animal zone (coops and barns), a southeast processing zone (kegs and Preserves Jars densely placed), and a north decoration zone. Putting the shipping bin right in the center of the planting zone can compress the daily walk from harvest to shipping to under 30 seconds. In the first year, prioritize developing the flat land near the river in the south—it's close to water, so you can water efficiently even without sprinklers at first.

Suited for: Beginners, all-round players, players who love planting. The Standard Farm is the "jack-of-all-trades" choice—whether you want a planting build, a wine-making build, an animal build, or a hybrid, it can carry it all perfectly. A saying circulates in the community: "If you don't know what to pick, pick Standard"—and that still holds true today.

🏞️ Riverland Farm

✅ Pros
  • Laid out along the river, fishing is extremely convenient
  • Free river water, no need to water
  • Unique scenery and atmosphere
⚠️ Notes
  • Limited farmland area
  • Space layout is constrained
  • Not suited for large-scale planting

Space utilization: The tillable area is only about 1,584 tiles, less than half of the Standard Farm. More troublesome is that the land is cut by the river into several small islands that must be connected by wooden bridges, making large sprinkler arrays hard to lay out. This means the Riverland Farm is naturally unsuited for large-scale planting builds.

Fishing strategy: The Riverland Farm's water is "pond"-type, and the fish you can catch differ from those in the town river—mainly catfish, rainbow trout, and largemouth bass. Catfish is a high-value fish in spring and fall (appearance rate affected by weather), and rainbow trout is a good source of golden-star bait in summer. We recommend a "fishing start + processing build": in the early game, fishing can earn several thousand G per day, much faster than planting parsnips; in the mid game, turn fish into Sashimi (recipe from Linus) or Smoked Fish (1.6's new smoker) to double the profit again. After year two, transition to a "small-scale planting + processing" boutique model to sidestep the space disadvantage.

Layout tips: Place kegs and Preserves Jars densely on the narrow islands near the house so you can process fish right after catching; concentrate the planting area on the largest central island, paired with pressure nozzles or quality sprinklers to save space. Reserve at least 2-tile-wide aisles to avoid being blocked by processing equipment.

Suited for: Fishing enthusiasts, casual players, players who pursue a unique visual experience. The Riverland Farm is not for players who pursue maximum efficiency, but for those who enjoy the slow-paced life of "fishing in the morning, planting flowers in the afternoon," it's one of the most beautiful farms.

🌲 Forest Farm

✅ Pros
  • Surrounded by forest, foraging and logging are convenient
  • Has a small pond for fishing
  • Beautiful natural environment
⚠️ Notes
  • Less flat land, limited planting space
  • Many trees, clearing is a hassle
  • Layout is relatively scattered

Space utilization: The tillable area is about 1,413 tiles, slightly smaller than Riverland. But the Forest Farm has lots of "bushes" and "stumps"—bushes refresh foraged items daily (leeks, wild plums, mushrooms, etc.), and stumps regenerate hardwood, a stable resource no other farm has. A player who keeps clearing can earn 3,000–5,000G per week just from foraging.

Foraging route: We recommend walking a fixed loop around the farm every morning: head north from the house to the forest edge, then loop east back around the pond, and finally return to the southern planting zone. This route takes about 1 minute of game time and yields 8–15 foraged items. If you take the Botanist branch (foraged items are always iridium quality), a single iridium wild plum can sell for 120G, easily surpassing 10,000G per week. The Forest Farm is the only farm that can make foraging a "main income source."

Logging value: The Forest Farm's hardwood stumps refresh every two days, each stump yields 1–2 hardwood. With about 8 stumps on the whole farm, that's a stable 4–6 hardwood per day. Hardwood is essential for building a stable (100) and expanding the house (50–150 per stage), with a market equivalent of 50G each. In other words, the Forest Farm can earn 200–300G per day from hardwood alone, completely passively.

Suited for: Foraging builds, nature lovers, players who enjoy slow-paced exploration. The Forest Farm is not for players who pursue maximum profit, but it's an excellent choice for casual players who "don't want to water 200 plants every day"—you can easily keep your planting area under 50 plants and live off foraging and fishing.

⛰️ Hilltop Farm

✅ Pros
  • Adjacent to the mine, mining is extremely convenient
  • Has open-air mining nodes you can dig directly
  • Can use the minecart for quick travel
⚠️ Notes
  • Mountain terrain, not flat
  • Smaller farmland area
  • Layout requires careful planning

Space utilization: The tillable area is only about 1,180 tiles, the smallest of the six farms. The terrain is cut by the mountain into terraced steps, with flat land scattered, so laying sprinklers requires pre-flattening a lot of land. But the Hilltop Farm has a unique "open-air ore vein"—rocks and ore nodes distributed across the farm surface that refresh every 4 days, including copper, iron, and gold ore, occasionally gems.

Mining build strategy: The Hilltop Farm's real value isn't "mining is close" (the town mine isn't far either), but rather that the "ore" from open-air veins directly bypasses mine combat. For players who aren't good at combat but need lots of ore to upgrade tools, this is a huge boon. Hammering a loop of rocks every morning can reliably yield 10–20 copper ore and 5–10 iron ore, enough to upgrade tools to steel by the first summer. Paired with the "Geologist" branch (gems appreciate 30%) you can also earn gem money on the side.

Layout challenges: The Hilltop Farm's biggest pain point is little flat land and broken terrain, making sprinklers hard to lay in sheets. We suggest a "terrace layout"—plan a separate planting area on each small platform, connected by stone paths. Reserve the flat area near the house for animal buildings and processing equipment to avoid wasting time running up and down. In the first year, don't chase large-scale planting—keep it under 60 plants and make up income with fishing and mining.

Suited for: Mining builds, explorer players, players who don't like combat but want ore. The Hilltop Farm is a "veteran challenge" choice, not recommended for beginners—broken terrain greatly amplifies the cost of operational mistakes.

🏜️ Wilderness Farm

✅ Pros
  • Wilderness environment, unique atmosphere
  • Has open-air ore veins
  • Monster spawns add challenge and drops
⚠️ Notes
  • Monsters spawn at night, requiring combat response
  • Harsh environment, harder management
  • High difficulty

Core mechanics: A common misconception needs clarifying—the Wilderness Farm "can only grow specific crops" is an early community myth. In reality, the Wilderness Farm can grow all crops normally, but its signature is that monsters spawn at night (golems, bats, slimes, etc.), and players must respond to combat at any time. This is also why its difficulty is rated ⭐⭐⭐⭐.

Monster response: Nighttime monster spawns consume a lot of player energy and time, but there are several countermeasures: ① build "torches" and "street lamps" to light up the farm—monsters won't spawn in lit areas; ② dig a moat around the house (use fences to fence off a monster-free zone); ③ choose the "Combat" skill line to boost attack power and clear monsters quickly; ④ just go to bed at night and clean up corpses the next day. A popular community playstyle is the "monster farm build"—deliberately letting monsters spawn and earning money from "Solar Essence" and "Void Essence" drops, paired with the "Monster Compendium" collection achievement.

Crop selection: Because of nighttime combat pressure, the Wilderness Farm isn't suited for multi-harvest crops that need meticulous care. We recommend "single-harvest + high-value" crops like cauliflower, melon, and pumpkin—plant them and only water occasionally, so forgetting to water one day won't cause heavy losses. Pomegranates and Cactus Fruit are actually products of the "desert" environment; the Wilderness Farm itself has no special crop bonus, so please don't be misled on this point.

Suited for: Challenge seekers, players who want a special playstyle, combat build enthusiasts. The Wilderness Farm is for players who are "tired of ordinary farming and want tension and excitement," not for players who pursue a peaceful rural life.

🏖️ Beach Farm

✅ Pros
  • Beach theme, beautiful scenery
  • Has tide pools that yield special items
  • Relatively large space
⚠️ Notes
  • Beach terrain, planting is restricted
  • Tide pools require special management
  • Not a traditional farm in the usual sense

Core mechanics: The Beach Farm is a special farm added in version 1.5. Its biggest feature is that sprinklers cannot be placed on sand—this is its biggest flaw. But the beach area is large, with a tillable area of about 2,942 tiles, second only to the Standard Farm. The contradiction is: the more you plant, the more daily manual watering energy you consume.

Tide pools: The tide pools on the east side of the Beach Farm refresh "tidal items" daily—conchs, shells, coral, seaweed, etc., occasionally rare items (like pearls, sea urchins). This is a passive income source no other farm has, stably yielding 200–500G per day. Tide pools can also catch ocean fish (like tuna, sardines, herring), equivalent to bringing "ocean fishing" home.

Layout strategy: Since you can't place sprinklers on the beach, you must concentrate the planting area on the "grass" part (a small flat area in the northwest corner of the farm). This grass is small but can use sprinklers normally, enough to support 100–150 crops. The beach is used for placing processing equipment (kegs, Preserves Jars), animal buildings, and decorations. This "grass planting + beach processing" zoning is the standard solution for the Beach Farm.

Suited for: Beach lovers, casual players, players who pursue aesthetics. The Beach Farm is the top choice for the "visual crowd"—wave sounds, sunsets, and shell decorations make it the most romantic farm. But efficiency players should choose carefully—the sprinkler restriction will make the late game very painful.

🔮 Long-term Impact and Community Reception

Choosing a farm isn't just choosing a piece of land—it's choosing a long-term experience. From community feedback and extensive player testing, the Standard Farm's advantage further widens from year three onward—because the core late-game gameplay is "keg matrix + massive crops," and the Standard Farm's large flat land can hold thousands of kegs and hundreds of wine-making ingredients, a scale advantage other farms can't match. This is also why speedrunners and million-gold achievement players almost universally choose the Standard Farm.

But there's also a "reverse selection" trend in the community: many veterans tired of the Standard Farm's efficiency-first approach turn to Forest, Riverland, or Beach to experience "slow living." The r/StardewValley subreddit on Reddit holds an annual "farm photo contest," and winning entries almost all come from Riverland, Forest, or Beach farms—their unique scenery is something the Standard Farm can't provide. In other words, the Standard Farm wins on efficiency, and the signature farms win on experience; both routes have devoted fans.

The debate over the "best farm" never ends, but the community has gradually reached a consensus: play the Standard Farm first (to learn the mechanics), then play a signature farm (for a personal experience). If you can only play one save and want to balance efficiency and experience, the Forest Farm is a compromise—it has enough planting space, unique passive resources, and scenery that rivals the Beach. It's a "not extreme in anything but passing in everything" choice.

Note that version 1.6 added the "Meadowlands Farm," which starts with two chickens and has blue grass for animals to graze on, suited for animal-husbandry players. It's not included in this comparison but is also worth a try.
